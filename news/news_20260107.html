Scientific discovery from the VENUS Program

Embargoed until January 7, 2026, 10:15 AM ET

The Strongly Lensed Supernova Pantheon as Revealed by JWST

Astronomers from the Space Telescope Science Institute (STScI) and the JWST Vast Exploration for Nascent, Unexplored Sources (VENUS) program have announced the discovery of two exceptionally rare events: strongly lensed supernovae whose light has been split into multiple images by the warping of spacetime.

Peering Through Nature’s Giant Telescopes

Massive objects in space, like galaxy clusters, can warp the fabric of spacetime—a phenomenon predicted by Einstein's General Relativity. When a cluster lies directly between Earth and a distant object, it acts as a "gravitational lens," magnifying and distorting the light from the background source.

The VENUS collaboration is currently using JWST to survey 60 such galaxy clusters. Even at the program’s midpoint, it has already uncovered rare phenomena including individual stars from the early Universe and stellar explosions that would otherwise remain invisible.

“Strong gravitational lensing transforms galaxy clusters into nature’s most powerful telescopes,” says Seiji Fujimoto, Principal Investigator of the VENUS program. “VENUS was designed to uncover the rarest events in the distant Universe, and these lensed supernovae are the perfect example of what this mission can achieve.”

A Predictive Experiment for Cosmic Expansion

SN Ares exploded when the Universe was only 4 billion years old. Because of the vast distances and the cluster's gravity, its various images will arrive at Earth decades apart. Astronomers predict the final images will not appear for another 60 years.

By measuring these "time delays," the VENUS team can precisely calculate the expansion rate of the Universe, addressing the so-called "Hubble Tension"—a major discrepancy in modern astronomy regarding how fast the Universe is growing.

“This system offers a predictive experiment that could place extraordinarily precise constraints on cosmological evolution,” says Conor Larison (STScI). “We have never seen such a long anticipated delay before.”

While SN Ares is a long-term study, the second discovery, SN Athena, is expected to reappear much sooner—within the next two years. Its reappearance will provide an immediate and independent measurement of cosmic expansion.
