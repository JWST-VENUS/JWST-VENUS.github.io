The Strongly Lensed Supernova Pantheon As Revealed by JWST

Astronomers from the Space Telescope Science Institute (STScI) and the JWST Vast Exploration for Nascent, Unexplored Sources (VENUS) Treasury program have discovered two exceptionally rare events: strongly lensed supernovae whose light has been split into multiple images by the bending of spacetime. These discoveries open a new avenue for studying how the Universe has evolved over cosmic time.

Peering Through Gravity’s Lenses

Any object with mass affects the curvature of spacetime, a central prediction of Einstein’s general theory of relativity. When a massive galaxy cluster lies between Earth and a distant background source, its gravity can warp spacetime so strongly that light from the background object is magnified and split into multiple distorted images.

Astronomers in the VENUS collaboration exploit this effect by using JWST to observe 60 massive galaxy clusters. Even though the program is only halfway complete, VENUS has already revealed rare and distant phenomena, including individual stars from the early Universe, active black holes in primordial galaxies, and stellar explosions invisible without gravitational lensing.

“Strong gravitational lensing transforms galaxy clusters into nature’s most powerful telescopes,” says Seiji Fujimoto, principal investigator of the VENUS survey. “VENUS was designed to uncover the rarest events in the distant Universe, and these lensed supernovae are exactly the kind of phenomena that only this approach can reveal.”

A Cosmological Experiment Decades in the Making

SN Ares exploded when the Universe was only about 4 billion years old. As its light travels through the expanding Universe, gravitational lensing causes its images to appear decades apart, with the final images predicted to arrive more than 60 years from now.

These long time delays transform SN Ares into a unique cosmological experiment. By precisely measuring the delays between images, astronomers can directly probe the expansion rate of the Universe and test competing measurements of the Hubble Constant.

“Such a long anticipated delay between images of a strongly lensed supernova has never been observed before,” says Conor Larison (STScI). “This system offers a predictive experiment that could place extraordinarily precise constraints on cosmological evolution.”

SN Athena, discovered at a later cosmic epoch, is expected to reappear within the next one to two years. Its relatively short delay will provide an independent and timely measurement of cosmic expansion.

“The upcoming reappearance of SN Athena will allow us to weigh in on the value of the Hubble Constant at a critical time,” says Justin Pierel (STScI). “It may point toward new physics or uncover previously unknown systematics.”

Together, SN Ares and SN Athena represent long-baseline experiments in cosmology, offering rare windows into how the Universe has evolved over billions of years — and reminding us that some of its most profound answers may still be on their way.
